Abstract: Storage-disaggregated databases decouple compute from storage, improving elasticity but suffering from network bottlenecks. Most cloud databases use page-based B+trees and buffer pools. Under disaggregation, each index traversal incurs serial network round-trips. Page-granularity transfers and caching also amplify network traffic and lower cache efficiency. Pushing down the full index to storage and caching only hot records at compute is appealing, but an efficient design must (1) provide a holistic record cache that accelerates both point and range queries, (2) mitigate the negative search penalty of confirming non-existence via remote probes, and (3) enforce phantom protection despite the index being encapsulated by the storage service.
  We introduce Nemo, a holistic index-and-buffer design optimized for storage-disaggregated OLTP with index pushdown. Nemo employs a tree-structured record buffer that unifies caching and buffering and accelerates both point and range queries. It further reduces the negative search penalty by caching negative search results. Nemo provides phantom protection that requires no knowledge of the storage-side data layout and is robust against large key domains, varying data skew, and partition schemes. Experiments show that Nemo outperforms page-based B+Tree and the state-of-the-art 2-Tree by up to 11.42x and 2.97x on positive point queries, and achieves up to 4.76x speedup on negative searches over a hash-indexed record cache. For phantom protection, Nemo outperforms PCL by up to 1.55x even under a dense key domain. Moreover, Nemo maintains stable performance while PCL degrades sharply as the key domain grows large and sparse.

Abstract: RDF 1.2 introduces statement-level constructs, such as triple terms, reifiers, and descriptions, that pose new challenges for transforming RDF data into labeled property graphs (LPGs). This work presents and compares three algorithms for translating RDF 1.2 data into LPGs, each interpreting a different amount of the RDF 1.2 reification vocabulary. Algorithm Structural interprets no vocabulary and losslessly supports arbitrary RDF 1.2 graphs by representing triple terms explicitly as nodes; Direct interprets rdf:reifies to recover annotated edges while preserving reifiers as graph objects; and Fold additionally folds reifier descriptions into edge properties, resulting in a more concise, albeit lossy, LPG representation. The experiments confirm the predicted trade-offs: all variants scale linearly on the tested workloads, the generality of Structural has no runtime penalty, and folding descriptions into edge records can significantly reduce the number of edges on description-heavy data.

Abstract: Nine years after learned indexes were proposed, their advantage on read-mostly data is well established, and a standard benchmark rates their best concurrent version as almost ready for use. Across more than 4000 runs of 13 indexes, we find that each design pays for concurrent inserts in a different way. Gapped-array indexes rebuild their nodes in synchronized bursts, at positions that follow from three constants in the source. Model-placement indexes use three to four times the memory, buffered indexes take two to three times as long. Three of five concurrent learned indexes lost keys or stopped making progress. Standard benchmarks miss both the bursts and the lost keys. The bursts cost far more than in B-trees because each rebuild copies a node a thousand times larger than a page and, under concurrency, blocks every operation that reaches the node. The typical B-tree fix of randomizing node density only spreads the bursts out,while smaller nodes trade them for longer run times at scale. Abstract: Geo-filtered approximate nearest-neighbor (GFANN) search retrieves the objects inside a query-specified geographic disk whose feature vectors are closest to a query vector, a core operation in location-aware Web search. A disk filter on a standard proximity graph invalidates out-of-disk routing vertices; on selective disks, these removals break greedy paths and recall drops sharply. Existing methods scan all in-disk objects, over-fetch unfiltered candidates, or maintain separate sub-indexes across spatial partitions, trading latency for index overhead. We propose REACH, a single-graph index based on row-local active-order invariance: if every vertex stores its outgoing neighbors in a fixed-width row sorted by vector distance, any disk filter masks out-of-disk entries while surviving in-disk (active) neighbors remain vector-sorted. Offline, each vertex merges globally closest vector candidates with a nearest-first geographic stream into one row; online, beam search evaluates vector distances only for the first l active entries of each visited row. Because co-located objects share the same distance to every query center, every disk admits a coordinate group in full or not at all, decomposing active connectivity into intra-group and inter-coordinate conditions. Under a spatial marked-site model with repeated coordinates, we prove that polylogarithmic out-degree guarantees strong connectivity across all disks and constant-factor distance reduction per hop on compact disks, while a cut-rank threshold characterizes the minimum scan budget l. Across four datasets up to 10^7 objects, one REACH index per dataset outperforms SOTA methods, achieving 2.49x higher geometric-mean QPS than the strongest baseline at 0.95 Recall@10 on selective queries, while building 3.8x-11.9x faster and using 37%-45% less space than a SOTA spatial-graph index.
