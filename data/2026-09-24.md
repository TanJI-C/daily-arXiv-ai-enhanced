Abstract: The realization of practical fault-tolerant quantum computation hinges on the efficient management of non-Clifford resources, which dominate both the temporal and spatial costs of executing quantum algorithms. Among the most consequential design decisions facing architects of error-corrected quantum systems is the trade-off between T-depth, the number of sequential layers of T-gates, and T-count, the total number of T-gates required by a circuit. These two metrics often pull in opposite directions: reducing T-depth through aggressive parallelization typically inflates the total T-count by requiring additional ancilla-assisted gate teleportation or redundant magic state preparation, while minimizing T-count through resource-efficient decompositions frequently serializes operations and thereby lengthens the critical path. Magic state factories, which produce the high-fidelity T-states consumed by non-Clifford gates, occupy a substantial fraction of the physical qubit budget in surface-code architectures. When T-depth is prioritized, factories must deliver states at a higher rate, necessitating either larger distillation complexes or multiple parallel factories, both of which increase qubit overhead and may introduce routing congestion that degrades effective parallelism. Conversely, when T-count is minimized, the reduced demand for magic states permits leaner factories, but the serialization of T-gate layers extends the wall-clock execution time, potentially by orders of magnitude for algorithms with deep circuits. These feedback effects mean that naive optimization of either metric in isolation can yield suboptimal resource profiles, and a holistic cost model is required to navigate the design space.
